Research on therapeutic intermittent fasts for treating obesity dates back to at least 1915, with renewed medical interest occurring in the 1960s after Bloom and his colleagues published an enthusiastic report. [1] These early clinical studies investigated short-term starvation periods ranging from 1 to 14 days. [1] In animal studies, calorie restriction has been shown to successfully increase lifespan and improve overall tolerance to metabolic stresses. [2] In human populations, research has evaluated whether intermittent fasting can lower the risk of diet-related conditions such as metabolic syndrome. [1] Although it may improve several health outcomes in overweight or obese adults, its effect on lowering blood pressure appears less pronounced than that of continuous energy restriction. [2] [⚑ [2] source doesn't say this] Additionally, research does not consistently demonstrate that intermittent fasting is superior to continuous low-calorie diets regarding overall weight loss efficiency. [2] Specific dietary methods evaluated in research include alternate-day fasting restricting intake to 25% of daily calorie needs, as well as the 5:2 diet which limits intake to 400–500 calories on two days of the week. [2] Regarding public safety, the United States National Institute on Aging states that current research remains limited and inconclusive, choosing not to recommend intermittent fasting to the general public. [1] Meanwhile, the New Zealand Ministry of Health notes that while the practice may aid weight loss, it can cause low energy and hunger, and it is explicitly not recommended for individuals with insulin-dependent diabetes.
